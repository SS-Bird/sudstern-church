# Kirche am Südstern: A Place Without a Place

Nov 11, 2026 · @Sophie

## Introduction

*A Place Without a Place* is an ergodic digital story about the Kirche am Südstern, a neo-Gothic church on a traffic island in Berlin-Kreuzberg. The reader can't read it straight through. They have to search, click, walk through Street View, choose, and sometimes get told off. The piece has no thesis. It walks the reader through a maze of ideas about who narrates a nation, and leaves them with questions.

The church was built between 1894 and 1897 to a design by Ernst August Roßteuscher. It was consecrated on 8 May 1897 in the presence of Kaiser Wilhelm II and Empress Auguste Viktoria.\[1\] Since then it has passed through many hands:

- the Reich treasury, which leased it for 30 years after 1918
- war damage in the Second World War
- the Berliner Stadtmission, until 1970
- the Serbian Orthodox Church, until 1981
- some years standing empty
- the Christliches Zentrum Berlin (CZB), which uses it today\[1\]

On its back wall hangs a headless First World War memorial by Hermann Hosaeus, unveiled on 1 June 1929.\[2\]

The story compares two sites that tell different narratives of the nation. The church is lived in, plural and still being rewritten by a bilingual, largely immigrant congregation. The memorial is fixed, ethno-symbolic and abandoned, though it is physically part of the same building. The church was monumental and imperial from the start, yet it sits off the tourist trail. That small step back from the centre is what let it keep collecting layers of Berlin's identities.

**Purpose.** The scene sets up the reader as a tourist, the outsider who consumes places. It also slips in the first theoretical thread. Michael Billig's *banal nationalism* is the idea that national identity is reproduced less by flag-waving than by the unnoticed flag hanging on a public building. It lives in the background reminders that tell people, every day and without their noticing, where they belong.\[4\] Brittany the blogger is not religious and barely notices Europe's Christianity. But every entry catches her bumping into it: in public holidays, street names, church tax, bells and football hymns. Europe has largely stepped back from church-going, yet the church keeps flagging a Christian identity at people from every corner. That background presence is part of what Europeans share, even as they formally detach from it.

**Purpose.** The reviews are how the church is normally narrated: when it was built, by whom, how tall, in what style. Each now opens a thread of replies, all from one account, **@DissemiNadia**. The replies draw on Homi K. Bhabha's argument that a nation is never one harmonious story. It is narrated constantly, by many voices that contest each other.\[5\] Bhabha separates the *pedagogical* voice from the *performative* one. The pedagogical voice teaches the nation as a finished history. The performative is the nation remade every day by the people living it. The replies argue that the past is still one of the voices, because it built itself into stone. The church's architecture, its protected status, and the formula we use to describe old buildings all keep the 1890s talking. The replies get more direct with each review.

### Unit A: the opening (any spot)

**LV:** You've probably already read the dates, 1894 to 1897. What's worth knowing is who showed up at the opening on 8 May 1897: Kaiser Wilhelm II and Empress Auguste Viktoria. On the same day they also consecrated the Johannes-Basilika, a Catholic church built just around the corner at the same time.\[1\]

**RV:** So the Kaiser opened two churches, one Protestant and one Catholic, side by side, on the same afternoon. That's a lot of effort for one square.

**LV:** It was deliberate. Berlin was growing fast, the empire was barely 25 years old, and putting the emperor at the altar told everyone that church, crown and state came as a package. **RV:** And it still looks like that. It doesn't look like it grew out of the neighbourhood. It looks like it was placed here on purpose.

### Unit B: the style (any spot)

**LV:** It's neo-Gothic: pointed arches, buttresses, everything pulling your eye up to a spire about 90 metres high.\[1\] That makes it one of the tallest churches in Berlin. Only the Berliner Dom is clearly taller, at 98.8 metres, and St. Marien in Mitte is about the same.\[2\]

**RV:** But it doesn't feel like the Dom at all. The Dom is all dome and weight. This one is narrow and sharp.

**LV:** That's the point of the style. Neo-Gothic copied the cathedrals of the Middle Ages, so a brand-new church in a brand-new empire could look as if it had always been there. It's a building claiming a past it didn't have yet.

**RV:** So it was dressed up as old from day one. It's an imitation of history that has now become actual history.

### Unit C: the tenants (any spot)

**LV:** After 1918 the building lost its original job, and the Reich treasury leased it for 30 years to keep it running as a Protestant church. It was damaged in the Second World War, then repaired for the Berliner Stadtmission, which held services here until 1970. After that it was leased to the Serbian Orthodox Church, which used it until 1981. Then it stood empty.\[1\]

**RV:** Empty? This huge thing, just sitting on the roundabout with nobody in it?

**LV:** For a while, until the Christliches Zentrum Berlin bought it, an alliance of free churches.\[1\] Its roots go back to the 1950s. Harold Herman, an American who used to be a Hollywood cameraman, held tent services on Potsdamer Platz, right on the sector border. In the 1970s young people dealing with addiction joined, as part of the Jesus People movement. In 1989 and 1990 the church opened its doors to East and West alike.\[3\]

**RV:** So it's had imperial Prussians, a city mission, Serbian Orthodox families, an empty decade, and an American revival that has grown into a congregation from all over the world. One building, at least five different Berlins.

## Scene 3.2: The pastors at the door

At the entrance, cardboard cutouts of the two pastors, David and Ben, pop up on either side of the screen. Each one's lines are highlighted in the colour of his shirt in the photo.

**Purpose.** This is the first look at the congregation, and it starts a thread the story returns to in Scene 3.3b. On the surface the community works like a *civic* nation. Michael Ignatieff defines civic nationalism as belonging by shared commitment rather than shared descent.\[7\] Here people with very different cultural backgrounds join one community by choice, under the banner of Christ rather than a flag. They become something like a band of brothers.

But under that civic surface, what Anthony D. Smith calls *ethno-symbolism*, the myths, memories and symbols that mark a people, is visible everywhere.\[6\] Every sermon is narrated in two voices. Usually a Black pastor preaches in English, with fire and movement. A white pastor translates into German, quieter, reserved, but just as engaged. Sometimes they swap. Older white German members dress the way you would picture people dressing for church: muted and formal. Many younger members, mostly African immigrants or their children, dress colourfully and dramatically, and worship expressively. One shared faith, two very visible ways of performing it. For now the voices only hint at this.

## Scene 3.3a: What answer? (the headless statue)

**Purpose.** This scene is about what *local* means. LV argues that the memorial is the most established member of the neighbourhood's collective, because nobody has been present longer. That is a civic argument: belonging through sustained participation. RV argues that the memorial embodies the opposite kind of nationalism. It is an ethno-symbol that marks belonging by blood, sacrifice and descent. Two secondary sources on war memorials give RV and LV their best arguments:

- **George L. Mosse** describes the *cult of the fallen soldier*. After 1918, war memorials borrowed Christianity's language of sacrifice and resurrection and gave it to the nation, so that dying for Germany looked like a sacred act.\[9\] A soldier memorial set into a church wall makes that fusion literal.
- **Reinhart Koselleck** argues that war memorials offer an identity to the survivors. Their meaning cannot stay fixed, because later generations read them in their own way.\[10\] The vandals who probably took a WWI pioneer for a Nazi soldier are exactly that kind of rereading.

## Scene 3.3b: Go to church

**Purpose.** The reader steps inside, first into a still photo of the interior and then into a sermon. The photo asks what the church is to most people who visit it: a working church, or a museum of a Christian Europe. Benedict Anderson argues that colonial states restored monuments and put them in museums. Those monuments became emblems of the state's role as guardian of a tradition, endlessly reproduced and rarely used.\[8\] Huge, beautiful and half-empty, Europe's churches now do similar work. They hold Christianity in place as a shared background for an imagined community,\[11\] frozen rather than lived. The sermon then brings back the Scene 3.2 thread about civic and ethno-symbolic nationalism.

## Glossary: Twerp's dictionary

These are the dictionary pages, in the order they are added. *Local* and *heritage* keep their placeholder dictionary definitions until a real dictionary entry and its citation are added. The rest are written plainly and point back to the theorist.

| Word | Added in | Definition |

| local | 3.3a, first dictionary call | Belonging to or connected with a particular place; a person who lives there, as opposed to a visitor. |
| civic nationalism | 3.3a, joint plot | Belonging to a nation through shared commitment to its laws, institutions and values, open in principle to anyone who joins.\[7\] |
| ethnic nationalism | 3.3a, with civic nationalism | Belonging to a nation through shared descent, language or culture: something you are born into rather than choose.\[7\] |
| ethno-symbolism | 3.3a, joint plot | Anthony D. Smith's view that nations are built from older myths, memories, symbols and values, which give a nation its sense of continuity with the past.\[6\] |
| heritage | 3.3a, "Flip the page" | Property, traditions or characteristics passed down from earlier generations; buildings and objects a community treats as worth keeping. |
| cult of the fallen soldier | 3.3a, Mosse | George L. Mosse's term for how war memorials after 1918 turned soldiers' deaths into a sacred sacrifice for the nation, borrowing Christian imagery.\[9\] |
| banal nationalism | Scene 4 or earlier (optional) | Michael Billig's term for national identity reproduced through everyday, unnoticed reminders, like the flag hanging on a public building rather than the one being waved.\[4\] |
| narration (of the nation) | Scene 2 profile (optional link) | Homi K. Bhabha's idea that a nation exists as a story told by many conflicting voices, split between a *pedagogical* official history and a *performative* everyday life.\[5\] |
