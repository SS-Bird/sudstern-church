# The Consciousness of a Place

Nov 11, 2026 · @Sophie

## Introduction

*The Consciousness of a Place* is an ergodic digital story about the Kirche am Südstern, a neo-Gothic church on a traffic island in Berlin-Kreuzberg. The reader can't read it straight through. They have to search, click, walk through Street View, choose, and sometimes get told off. It doesn't have a direct thesis. It walks the reader through a maze of ideas about who narrates a nation, and leaves them with the ambivalence natural to nationalism and collective identities.

The church was built between 1894 and 1897 to a design by Ernst August Roßteuscher. It was consecrated on 8 May 1897 in the presence of Kaiser Wilhelm II and Empress Auguste Viktoria.¹ Since then it has passed through many hands:

- the Reich treasury, which leased it for 30 years after 1918
- war damage in the Second World War
- the Berliner Stadtmission, until 1970
- the Serbian Orthodox Church, until 1981
- some years standing empty
- the Christliches Zentrum Berlin (CZB), which uses it today²

On its back wall hangs a headless First World War memorial by Hermann Hosaeus, unveiled on 1 June 1929.³

The project's framework is Homi K. Bhabha's account of the nation as *narration*. For Bhabha the nation is never one continuous story. It is an ambivalent, contested telling, carried out by many voices at once, inside a "transitional social reality" where "the margins of the nation displace the centre."⁴ The site is built as that kind of narration. Its form is deliberately chaotic: two bickering narrators, reviews and replies, out-of-order history, branching paths. Bhabha writes of "a particular ambivalence that haunts the idea of the nation, the language of those who write of it and the lives of those who live it."⁵ The form is meant to perform that ambivalence, not just describe it.

The church is a powerful cultural object that lasts far longer than any one social order. Through that longevity, the people who built it keep inscribing their part in the narration, even after a century of ruptures between them and the present. Today a bilingual, largely immigrant congregation fills it. In Bhabha's terms, it embodies "the new realities of internationalism, multi-nationalism," yet those realities are "underwritten" by older power.⁶ Here that power is the inertia of an imperial, ethno-symbolic building that still gives the congregation its roof. The headless memorial on the back wall is a second, abandoned narrator, fixed in 1929, comparing two narratives of the nation on one building.

**Purpose.** The scene serves as a light introduction and sets up the reader as a tourist, the outsider who consumes places.

It also slips in the first theoretical thread. Michael Billig's *banal nationalism* is the idea that national identity is reproduced less by flag-waving than by the unnoticed flag hanging on a public building. It lives in the background reminders that tell people, every day and without their noticing, where they belong.⁸ Europe has largely stepped back from church-going, yet the church keeps flagging a Christian identity at people from every corner. That background presence is part of what Europeans share, even as they formally detach from it.

A morphed call to Christianity also enters the nationalism of Europe, as its old Christian monuments become important tourism commodities and an international audience voices its understanding of Europe. What's externally legible is maybe more defining than ever, given modern global movement and conversation.

Banal nationalism isn't really "the point" of this experience. If the reader misses it, that's okay, but it is relevant, and it contributes to the mess of nationalism.

### Unit A: the opening (a spot along the side)

**LV:** You've probably already read the dates, 1894 to 1897. Kaiser Wilhelm II and Empress Auguste Viktoria opened it on 8 May 1897. On the same day they also consecrated the Johannes-Basilika, a Catholic church built just around the corner at the same time.¹⁷

**RV:** So the Kaiser opened two churches, one Protestant and one Catholic, side by side, on the same afternoon. That's a lot of effort for one square.

**LV:** It was deliberate. Berlin was growing fast, the empire was barely 25 years old, and putting the emperor at the altar told everyone that church, crown and state came as a package. The architect, Ernst August Roßteuscher, was a state building official.¹⁸ That makes this a government building as much as a religious one.

### Unit B: the Gothic (the front of the church)

**LV:** It's neo-Gothic: pointed arches, buttresses, everything pulling your eye up to a spire about 90 metres high.¹⁹ Look at what that does. The German Empire was barely 25 years old when this went up, and it dressed itself in the Middle Ages. In that era, picking a style was picking a story about the nation. When the new empire argued over what style its Reichstag should be, classical meant a centralised state like Rome, and Gothic meant the Middle Ages: spread-out power, old parliaments. The historian Michael Lewis puts the stakes simply: "what Germany chose to find in the past would signal her aspirations for the future." A brand-new state referencing a proud "ancient" history. In their Warwick debate, Ernest Gellner and Anthony Smith argued over whether nations have "navels": real roots in an ethnic past, or modern inventions that only look as if they were born. Gellner thought a nation could be fully modern without one. The Gothic is the empire drawing its own navel on.²⁰

**RV:** But why Gothic? Plenty of Berlin is all Greek columns and domes. The Berliner Dom looks nothing like this.

**LV:** Because Gothic spent the whole century being fought over. In the 1840s and 50s a Catholic politician from the Rhineland, August Reichensperger, campaigned for Gothic as the true art of the German people, rooted in a "healthy folk instinct" and surviving in village carpentry.²¹ His enemy was Berlin. He told a Berlin audience: "Berlin has no folk art. She only has court art," imitation Greek, "out of place in the cold north."²² Then the Protestant north took his style and made it national. Lutherans came to see Gothic "in chiefly nationalist terms," and in 1861 the church rules drawn up at Eisenach brought Gothic into Protestant church building.²³ But actually, by the time this church opened, it was already a little out of date after its peak fashionability in the 80s. Wilhelm II, the Kaiser who opened it, preferred round-arched Romanesque. The nationalist feeling Gothic used to carry was, in Lewis's words, "being drained toward Romanesque building."²⁴

**RV:** So a style the margins used against the capital ended up as the capital's own costume. A Catholic, small-town, "folk" style, built here by a state architect and opened by the Kaiser.

**LV:** The story never stops changing hands.

**RV:** And now look who fills it on Sunday. It's changing hands again.

### Unit C: the tenants (a spot along the side)

**LV:** After 1918 the building lost its original job, and the Reich treasury leased it for 30 years to keep it running as a Protestant church. It was damaged in the Second World War, then repaired for the Berliner Stadtmission, which held services here until 1970. After that it was leased to the Serbian Orthodox Church, which used it until 1981. Then it stood empty.²⁵

**RV:** Empty? This huge thing?

**LV:** For a while, until the Christliches Zentrum Berlin bought it, an alliance of free churches.²⁶ Its roots go back to the 1950s. Harold Herman, an American who used to be a Hollywood cameraman, held tent services on Potsdamer Platz, right on the sector border. According to their website, in the 1970s young people dealing with addiction joined as part of the Jesus People movement, and then in 1989 and 1990 the church opened its doors to East and West alike.²⁷

**RV:** So it's had imperial Prussians, a city mission, Serbian Orthodox families, an empty decade, and an American revival that has grown into a congregation from all over the world.

## Scene 3.3a: What answer? (the headless statue)

**Purpose.** This scene is about what *local* means. LV argues that the memorial is the most established member of the neighbourhood's collective, because nobody has been present longer. That is a civic argument: belonging through sustained participation. RV argues that the memorial embodies the opposite kind of nationalism. It is an ethno-symbol that marks belonging by blood, sacrifice and descent. The same object is told two ways, and neither telling wins. The scene then turns to Germany's military and violent past. Much of it is either deliberately neglected or carried as guilt, and an abandoned symbol stays open for violent groups in the present to recruit into their own version of the nation. Two secondary sources on war memorials give RV and LV their best arguments:

- **George L. Mosse** describes the *cult of the fallen soldier*. After 1918, war memorials borrowed Christianity's language of sacrifice and resurrection and gave it to the nation, so that dying for Germany looked like a sacred act.²⁸ A soldier memorial set into a church wall makes that fusion literal.
- **Reinhart Koselleck** argues that war memorials offer an identity to the survivors. Their meaning cannot stay fixed, because later generations read them in their own way.²⁹

The dictionary grows here. Each time a dense term comes up, a new page and tab are added.

## Scene 3.3b: Go to church

### Part 0: the pastors at the door

*The reader is moved in Street View to the front of the church, by the entrance. Cardboard cutouts of the two pastors, David and Ben, pop up on either side of the screen. Each one's lines are highlighted in the colour of his shirt in the photo.*

In many ways the community works like a *civic* nation, belonging by shared commitment rather than shared descent. That is the contrast Anthony D. Smith draws between civic nations, defined by territory and law, and ethnic ones, defined by descent and culture. Eric Hobsbawm adds that nations are largely made from above, by states and elites, but can't be understood without also looking from below, at the hopes and needs of ordinary people. The church was built from above. This congregation is the view from below.³⁶ Here people of very different backgrounds join one community by choice, under the banner of Christ rather than a flag. Sometimes they swap who's translating, but mostly it's English first. Older white German members dress in muted, formal church clothes. ### #ah152-CollectiveIdentitiesInHistory

I started out intending to make a messy experience out of many of the theorists, and then gradually realised that, in some ways, the mess was the point of Bhabha. I leaned into him more. Reading him made much more sense once I attached him to an example; the church grounded his very literary and abstract style. I used his account of the nation as an ambivalent, contested narration as the framework, and treated the other theorists as narrators within it. Each one historicises a different source of collective identity attached to this one building:

- the Prussian state's choice of a medieval style (Lewis, read through the Smith–Gellner debate on whether nations have ethnic "navels")
- the cult of the fallen soldier after 1918 (Mosse, Koselleck)
- banal Christian reminders in secular Europe (Billig)
- a chosen, bilingual, largely immigrant congregation (Smith, Hobsbawm)

The project also assesses those approaches. The congregation doesn't fit Smith's civic/ethnic contrast, because it is both at once, which is exactly the ambivalence Bhabha predicts. And LV's argument that the statue is the most "local" member of the neighbourhood tests how far a civic definition of belonging can stretch. The Gothic church sits right in the middle of the Smith–Gellner debate: it is a modern state inventing an ancient-looking past, which supports Gellner's modernism, yet the style only worked because it drew on real medieval memory, as Smith would insist. Hobsbawm's point that nations are made from above but have to be read from below is why I put the congregation's experience beside the state's building. Only after the project was basically done did I read some of "DissemiNation" and realise it would also have been very relevant. I might have been able to connect my use of *local* to his use of *locality*, and done more with temporality.

### #ah152-HistoryScales

The project deliberately moves between scales and lets the reader feel what each one shows and hides.

- **Geographic:** it starts at the scale of a single building and its back wall. It widens through the walk around the traffic island and Kreuzberg, then to Berlin as a city of arrivals and departures. Scene 4 makes this literal: the map zooms out from the church's footprint to Germany until the church is a red speck.
- **Temporal:** the walk's history units cover 1894 to today in fragments, out of order. The memorial freezes one moment, 1929, while the sermon happens in the present.
- **Thematic:** the same site is read through architecture, war memory, religion, migration and tourism.

The trade-offs are part of the argument. Zoomed in, the congregation's bilingual service looks like a living, plural community. Zoomed out, the church is one small instance of how Germany and Europe still carry Christianity as background identity. The building-level scale gives concreteness and lived detail, but it can't show how representative this congregation is of Berlin. The national scale shows patterns, like Gothic as a contested national style or the neglect of military memorials, but it loses the individual people in the pews.

### #ah152-IdeasAndSociety

The clearest case is the Gothic. Using Lewis, I situated the neo-Gothic style of the church within nineteenth-century political and religious practice:

- Reichensperger's Catholic, regionalist campaign for Gothic against Berlin's "court art"
- Protestant Prussia adopting Gothic in nationalist terms after the 1861 Eisenach rules
- the Reichstag style debate, where choosing a style meant choosing a story about the state
- Wilhelm II's turn to Romanesque, which left this church slightly out of date when it opened

I connected the ideas in the sermon (that you are your consciousness, and produce what comes from it) to the social practice of the bilingual service I attended. Through LV and RV, I also connected them to Marx's claim that social being shapes consciousness. The war memorial shows ideas turned into material practice: Hosaeus helped write the state guidelines for war memorials, and the memorial applies the cult of the fallen soldier in stone. Its later neglect and vandalism show those ideas being contested in present-day social practice.

### #medium and #critique

The church communicates through its medium before anyone says a word. Its neo-Gothic style claims a medieval past for an empire barely 25 years old. Its scale (a 90-metre spire, room for a thousand people) argues for power and permanence. Its siting on a traffic island makes the whole neighbourhood literally steer around it. The memorial is a different medium: a larger-than-life relief bolted to the back wall, speaking through iconography (oak leaves, Iron Crosses, a pioneer's tools and grenades) and through its inscription, "Vorwärts und durch."

My critique of these sources is built into the project's two narrators:

- **The building's message has shifted.** It was meant to fuse crown, church and nation. Today it is mostly seen from outside, as architecture, while inside it holds a congregation its builders would not have pictured.
- **The memorial's meaning has drifted too.** Its head has been missing since at least 2008, and it is neglected and repeatedly painted, most likely by people reading it as a Second World War symbol. That is Koselleck's point about memorials outliving the identity they were made to give.
- **My other sources have limits:**
  - The church website is self-presentation.
  - Wikipedia and the reviews repeat the same "built in, by, in what style" formula.
  - The Digital Cosmonaut post is a well-researched blog, not scholarship.
  - Lewis's book ends in 1895 and does not discuss this church directly.

  I used each one for what it can support.

### #levelsofanalysis

The project analyses the site at several levels and shows how they interact:

- **Individuals:** Kaiser Wilhelm II, the architect Roßteuscher, Reichensperger, Hosaeus, the two pastors, and the reader, whom the final questions address directly.
- **Groups:** Catholics and Protestants arguing over the Gothic; successive congregations from the Stadtmission to the Serbian Orthodox Church to the CZB; within today's congregation, older white German members and younger African members.
- **Institutions:** the Prussian state, the church, the city that does not maintain the memorial.
- **The nation:** Germany's narration of itself, its military past, and Europe's Christian background.

These levels interact, which is why the project keeps moving between them. A state architect's style choice shapes how individual visitors still describe the building. A congregation's choices as a group re-narrate a national monument. The neglect of one memorial raises a national question about who gets to tell the story of Germany's violent past.
